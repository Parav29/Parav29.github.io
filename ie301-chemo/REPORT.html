Day-care chemotherapy unit: dataset, formulation and first results

What we built after the Tata Memorial visit: a synthetic dataset traced to published sources, a simulation of the 10-step flow, an integer programme that sets two times for every patient, and the checks that each part does what it claims.

1What was done

  1. Searched for data. Kaggle and the literature have no public per-patient timestamps for an infusion unit. We built a synthetic dataset instead, with every input tied to a paper or to an assumption from Presentation 3.
  2. Verified the data against its own specification and against the Presentation 3 numbers.
  3. Built a discrete-event simulation (Python, SimPy) of the 10-step flow the TMH doctors confirmed, and verified it.
  4. Wrote the optimisation model: a chance-constrained booking rule plus a time-indexed integer programme for chair slots. We verified it against brute force.
  5. Ran the experiments from slide 10 on 100 identical simulated days, reporting 95% confidence intervals. E1 and E2 are complete; E3–E6 are still running.
  6. Prepared the AnyLogic version: a build guide and a data workbook, so AnyLogic replays exactly the same days.

Code, data and figures are in the ie301-chemo/ folder on branch claude/busy-pascal-b1i9cm.

2The dataset

Every patient is drawn first, and then every stage time is drawn for that patient. Each day has its own seed, so every booking rule is tested on exactly the same days (common random numbers). The class is set by comparing one stored random number with the pre-cleared share, so changing that share changes nothing else about the day.

Patients are added until the planned chair time fills 60% of the 20 chairs × 10 hours. Planned chair time is setup plus planned infusion plus takedown.

Unit (base case)

20 chairs · 8 nurses · one nurse sets up 1 patient or monitors 4 · 2 registration desks · 2 doctors · 3 pharmacy hoods · open 08:00–18:00 · bookings fill 60% of chair-hours.

Presentation 3 assumed 80% load. The simulation shows that 80% is infeasible: same-day patients cannot be ready before about 11:30, so overtime stays above 2 hours under every rule, even with 10 nurses. The base case is therefore 60%. 80% stays in as a stress test.

4The formulation

Three levels, solved in the order the decisions are made. The simulation scores the result.

Level 1  Capacity       chairs K, nurses N        grid search, scored by simulation   (E6)
Level 2  Booking rule   buffer B_c = F_c⁻¹(q)      chance constraint (newsvendor)       (E2, E3)
Level 3  Daily slots    chair slot s_i per patient time-indexed integer programme       (solved each day)
         ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
         Evaluation     simulated day, 100 seeded days, 95% confidence intervals

Level 0: problem statement (Presentation 1)

minimise cK·K + cN·N + cO·E[overtime]
subject to P90(wait) ≤ W ,   patients treated per day ≥ λ

Level 2: two times per patient (chance constraint)

ai is the arrival time told to the patient. si is the chair slot. Di is the random delay from told arrival to drug ready.

  • Pre-cleared: D = lateness + registration + billing + pharmacy.
  • Same-day: add blood draw, lab and doctor review.
require P( ai + Di ≤ si ) ≥ q  ⟺  si − ai ≥ Fc−1(q) = Bc
choose ai = si − Bc   (any extra gap is pure patient waiting)
q = 0.8 gives BP = 89.9 → 90 min,  BS = 206.2 → 210 min

The buffer is a newsvendor quantile. If one idle chair-minute costs cidle and one patient waiting-minute costs cwait, the best q is cidle / (cidle + cwait). So choosing q is choosing how much an empty chair matters compared with a waiting patient.

Level 3: daily chair-slot integer programme

The grid has 15-minute slots t = 0 … T−1 from 08:00, with closing at slot Tc (18:00) and 4 hours of possible overtime after it.

Patients with the same class, planned infusion and release are interchangeable. They form a group g of size ng, needing Lg chair slots: 2 for setup, the infusion, and 1 for takedown.

The nurse load of a group-g patient in their k-th chair slot is ωg,k: M during setup and takedown, 1 during infusion.

variables yg,t ∈ ℤ≥0 : patients of group g starting at slot t  (t ≥ rg = ⌈Bc/15⌉)
zτ ∈ {0,1} : unit still open at slot τ

minimise Σg,t (t + Lg)·yg,t  +  wm·Στ zτ  +  wo·Στ≥Tc zτ
(flowtime + makespan + overtime;  wm = n/4,  wo = 10n)

(C1) booked Σt yg,t = ng   ∀g
(C2) chairs Σg Σt ≤ τ < t+Lg yg,t ≤ K·zτ   ∀τ
(C3) nurses Σg Σt ≤ τ < t+Lg ωg,τ−t·yg,t ≤ M·N   ∀τ
(C4) buffer yg,t = 0 for t < rg
(C5) open zτ ≥ zτ+1   ∀τ
(C6) pharmacy Σg Σt=ττ+1 yg,t ≤ ⌊H·30 / p̄⌋   ∀τ  (optional)

Solving.

  • Size: about 560 variables and 950 constraints for a 50-patient day.
  • Solver: HiGHS, started from the best of 42 greedy schedules, with a 15 s limit and a 1% gap. The mean solve time is 6.9 s.
  • Earlier version: the first formulation used a binary for each (group, start) pair linked to makespan. It did not converge in 60 s. Putting zτ into the chair constraint (C2) is what makes the model fast.
  • Why not one big stochastic model: Hadid 2022 searched every appointment time inside a simulation and needed about 13 hours for 246 appointments. Here the randomness sits in two numbers, BP and BS.

5Verification of the formulation, including brute force

Brute-force search: on small instances, every possible combination of start times is enumerated, the infeasible ones are discarded, and the best objective is recorded. The result is flat. Patients who are ready early may start early (C10), so the schedule absorbs a wrong buffer. A larger buffer mainly makes patients less late for their own slot. q = 0.65 and 0.80 round to the same 15-minute buffers.

Bottlenecks

  1. Ready time is the biggest loss: about 37% of chair-hours are lost because the patient is in the unit but not yet ready. Same-day patients cannot reach a chair before about 11:30. The fix is pre-clearance; E3 measures how much it helps.
  2. Nurses are the binding resource in the afternoon, not chairs.
  3. The front desk and doctors are swamped only when everyone arrives at 08:00 (registration queue 88 min). Any staggered arrival times remove this.
  4. Pharmacy is not a bottleneck at 3 hoods: queues are 2–3 min.

  • Overtime has a knee at about 40–60% pre-cleared. Under the MIP it falls 100 → 48 → 27 → 15 min, then only 8 → 3.5 min. Beyond about 60%, extra phone triage barely shortens the day.
  • Waiting keeps improving all the way to 100% under the MIP (238 → 125 min), because every pre-cleared patient skips about 2 h of blood test, lab and review.
  • Under "everyone at 08:00" the wait flattens after about 60% (271 → 250 min): the morning crowd at registration dominates. Pre-clearance pays off fully only together with staggered arrival times.
  • The best schedule changes shape: at 0% pre-cleared, no chair can be used before about 11:30, so overtime is 100 min even with the MIP. At 60%, the morning chairs fill with pre-cleared patients.
  • Practical target: pre-clear about 60% of patients, combined with the two-time rule. That gives 15 min overtime and a 178 min mean wait, against 127 min and 316 min today (E1).

  • Process changes beat extra capacity. Pre-clearing 60% and pre-compounding their drugs cuts overtime from 37 to 10 min and mean wait from 213 to 157 min, with no new staff or chairs.
  • More chairs do nothing, because chairs are not the constraint.
  • A third registration desk is the best single resource to add.
  • More nurses made things worse. This is a finding about our formulation, not about nurses. The MIP models chairs and nurses but not the front desk. With 10 nurses it packs more patients into the earliest slots, so more people are told the same arrival time. The registration queue doubles (13 → 28 min) and so does the doctor queue (10 → 20 min). Patients then miss their slots and the day runs late. The bottleneck moves to the front desk. Constraint C7 (below) addresses this.

Running. C7 caps told arrivals per 15 minutes at what the registration desks (3 per 15 min) and doctors (2 same-day per 15 min) can serve. E7 reruns the E5 fixes and the E6 grid with it. This section will be filled in when it finishes.

8Assumptions to review

  • Load 60% instead of 80%, because 80% is infeasible (section 2).
  • 8 nurses, about 6 patients per nurse per shift. There is no ONS or NABH ratio; published ranges are 3–20, and the Cleveland Clinic target is 1:6.
  • Early starts allowed (C10). This is why buffer size barely matters; a "no early start" run is a fair next test.
  • Takedown needs a whole nurse (from Athawale), and billing is a delay, not a queue.
  • All step times from Presentation 3 are kept unchanged, so results stay consistent with what was presented.
  • Published figures not yet checked against full text. They came from abstracts and search excerpts, because the build environment could not open journal pages. Check them before quoting on slides.
